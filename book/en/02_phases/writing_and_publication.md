# Phase 3. Writing and publication

An SLR paper has a fixed structure and reviewers look for it: if the flow diagram, the search strings or the limitations are missing, rejection is almost certain. Write with the checklist (PRISMA 2020 or SEGRESS) at hand and complete it before submitting; many journals request it as supplementary material.

## Recommended structure (Kitchenham et al., 2015; PRISMA 2020)

| # | Section | Content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Title | Topic and type of review ("systematic review", "systematic mapping study", "meta-analysis") |
| 2 | Abstract | Structured: background, objectives, method (sources, dates, criteria, number of studies), results, conclusions (PRISMA 2020 for Abstracts, 12 items) |
| 3 | Introduction | Rationale (which reviews exist and why this one is needed), objectives and questions |
| 4 | Background | What is needed to understand the topic and the key concepts |
| 5 | Method | Where the protocol is; sources, strings and dates; eligibility criteria; selection process (how many reviewers, how disagreements were resolved, tools used); extraction; quality assessment; synthesis method. Decide what goes in the body and what goes in the appendix |
| 6 | Results | PRISMA diagram with the number of studies excluded at each phase; characteristics of the included studies; quality and biases; synthesis by question with tables and figures |
| 7 | Discussion | Answer to each question, implications for research and practice, and limitations |
| 8 | Conclusions | Overall interpretation and future work |
| 9 | Acknowledgements and declarations | Funding, conflicts of interest, use of AI if any |
| 10 | Appendices and supplementary material | Strings by source, extraction form, list of studies excluded at full text with reasons, quality checklist, extracted data |

If you have carried out both a mapping study and an SLR, the paper usually has a results section for each; each section answers its own questions and uses charts, tables and visual explanations. Pautasso's (2013) ten rules still hold: define the topic and audience, take notes while reading, keep the review focused but of broad interest, be critical and consistent, find a logical structure, make use of feedback, include your own research objectively, and stay up to date without forgetting older studies.

## Limitations and threats to validity

An honest SLR declares its threats and what it did to mitigate them. The four classic ones in software engineering (Ampatzoglou et al., 2019) are:

- **Construct validity**: do the search terms and criteria capture the concept under study? Mitigation: control set, synonyms, piloting.
- **Internal validity**: were screening and extraction carried out without bias? Mitigation: two reviewers, kappa, registered protocol.
- **External validity**: do the results generalise? It depends on the coverage of sources, languages and years; the publication bias of the primary studies cannot be eliminated.
- **Reliability**: would another team obtain the same results? Mitigation: published strings, dates and data.

SEGRESS asks for this section to be called "limitations"; the content is the same.

## Where to publish

An SLR can be a chapter of a master's thesis or PhD thesis, a journal article, a conference paper or a book chapter. To choose a journal, look at where recent SLRs on your topic have been published (many journals have their own *surveys* or *reviews* section) and prepare the manuscript following the style of that journal's latest review articles. If the review is very large, publish one part and leave the rest for another paper or for the thesis dissertation.

## What to expect from peer review

The most common comments on an SLR: the search is not reproducible; an obvious source is missing; a single reviewer did the screening; there is no quality assessment; the synthesis is a list; threats to validity are not discussed; the paper is too long. Several of these can be anticipated with the checklist; the last one, by moving tables to the supplementary material. Bear in mind too that not all reviewers know the method well: briefly explain why a mapping study does not assess quality or why there is no meta-analysis.
